1What Differentiation Really Means: Gradient of a Tangent

At its core, differentiation finds the "instantaneous" gradient of a curve at a single point — the gradient of the tangent line there. Imagine a chord (secant line) connecting two nearby points on a curve. As those two points move closer and closer together, the secant line rotates into the tangent line. That limiting process is exactly what the derivative dy/dx represents.

The tangent at a point (solid teal line) — the secants (dashed purple lines) approach it as the second point moves closer
dy/dx = lim(h→0) [f(x+h) − f(x)] / h This is the "first principles" definition of a derivative — understanding it clarifies exactly what differentiation is calculating

3The Chain Rule

When a function is "a function of a function" (a composite function) — like y = sin(3x) or y = (2x+1)⁵ — the chain rule is needed. The most intuitive way to think about it is "peeling an onion": differentiate the outer layer first, leave the inner layer untouched, then multiply by the derivative of the inner layer.

If y = f(g(x)), then dy/dx = f'(g(x)) × g'(x) Differentiate the outside, keep the inside as-is, then multiply by the derivative of the inside
"Onion" diagram: for y = sin(3x), the outer layer is sin( ) and the inner layer is 3x
Worked example

Differentiate y = (2x+1)⁵ Outer: u⁵ → 5u⁴; Inner: u=2x+1 → du/dx=2 dy/dx = 5(2x+1)⁴ × 2 = 10(2x+1)⁴

⚠ Common mistake: differentiating only the outer layer and forgetting to multiply by the inner derivative (writing dy/dx as 5(2x+1)⁴ and dropping the final ×2) — this is the single most common, and most deceptively "looks-right", chain rule error.

4The Product Rule and Quotient Rule

When two functions are multiplied together (y = uv) or divided (y = u/v), you cannot simply differentiate each one separately and combine them — a dedicated rule is needed for each case.

Product Rule: dy/dx = u(dv/dx) + v(du/dx) Cross-multiply each function with the other's derivative, then add
Quotient Rule: dy/dx = [v(du/dx) − u(dv/dx)] / v² Order matters: v·u' MINUS u·v', all over v² — reversing the order flips the sign of the whole answer
Worked example (Product Rule)

Differentiate y = x²sin(x) u=x², v=sin(x) → u'=2x, v'=cos(x) dy/dx = x²cos(x) + 2x·sin(x)

⚠ Common mistake: when v itself is a composite function (e.g. v=sin(2x)), finding v' still requires the chain rule (v'=2cos(2x), not cos(2x)) — the two rules very often need to be stacked together.

5Differentiating Trigonometric Functions

The rules for differentiating trig functions aren't hard to memorise — the real difficulty is that exam questions almost always embed a trig function inside a composite function (needing the chain rule), or multiply/divide it with something else (needing the product/quotient rule). Get into the habit of identifying the structure first, then choosing the rule.

d/dx [sin(kx)] = k·cos(kx)　　d/dx [cos(kx)] = −k·sin(kx) For a constant k, the chain rule simply brings k out as a multiplier
Worked example

Differentiate y = cos²(x)　(i.e. (cosx)²) Treat as a chain rule problem: y=u², u=cosx dy/dx = 2cosx × (−sinx) = −2sinx cosx　(can be further simplified to −sin(2x))

⚠ Common mistake: cos²x means "(cosx)²" — a completely different function from cos(x²). Always read the position of the exponent carefully.

6Application 1: Tangents and Normals

A tangent touches a curve at exactly one point, with gradient equal to the derivative there. A normal is perpendicular to the tangent, so its gradient is the negative reciprocal of the tangent's gradient.

Tangent: y − f(a) = f'(a)(x − a)　　Normal: y − f(a) = [−1/f'(a)](x − a)
The tangent (teal) and normal (purple) at the same point — the two lines are perpendicular
⚠ Common mistake: substitute a into the ORIGINAL function f(x) to get the y-coordinate, but into the DERIVATIVE f'(x) to get the gradient — these two are easy to mix up mid-calculation.

7Application 2: Stationary Points and Classification

A stationary point is where dy/dx = 0 — it could be a local maximum, a local minimum, or a point of inflection. The sign of the second derivative d²y/dx² lets you classify it quickly.

d²y/dx² < 0 → maximum　　d²y/dx² > 0 → minimum　　d²y/dx² = 0 → inconclusive, check the gradient either side
A local maximum and local minimum on a curve, with dashed lines marking where the tangent gradient is zero
⚠ Common mistake: stopping after finding the x-value — most questions require the full coordinates (x, y), and forgetting to substitute back into the original function is a very common lost mark.

📌 Key Takeaways

  • Differentiation is geometrically a tangent's gradient — when unsure of an answer, recalling that "dy/dx just measures how steep the curve is at that point" helps you sanity-check it.
  • The chain rule is "peeling an onion": differentiate the outer layer, leave the inner layer as-is, then multiply by the inner layer's derivative — this is the universal mental model for every composite-function differentiation.
  • The product rule has no minus sign; the quotient rule does — the two look structurally similar but the signs are easy to confuse, so recite the formula mentally before starting.
  • Trig derivatives only hold in radians — this links directly to the Trigonometry and Modelling guide, so cross-review the two together.
  • Substitute the point into the original function for coordinates, into the derivative for gradient — this mix-up is the most common way marks are lost on tangent/normal questions.
  • Write stationary point answers in full: not just the x-value, but the y-coordinate too, plus a clear classification ("maximum" or "minimum") backed by the second derivative.
  • Combining with Trigonometry: many synoptic questions simplify a trig expression into R sin(θ+α) form first, then either differentiate or read off the max/min directly — spotting the "no differentiation needed" shortcut saves significant time.
