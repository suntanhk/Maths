1Why Radians?

Degrees are an arbitrary human convention (splitting a circle into 360 equal parts), while a radian is defined directly from the circle's own geometry — as the angle for which the arc length equals the radius. This is exactly why every standard calculus result (like d/dx(sinθ) = cosθ) only holds in radians: their derivations rely on the approximation sinθ ≈ θ for small θ, which is only accurate in radian measure.

radians = degrees × (π / 180) e.g. 180° = π rad, 90° = π/2 rad, 60° = π/3 rad
The unit circle, with common angles labelled in both degrees and radians
⚠ Common mistake: when using the arc length formula s = rθ or the sector area formula A = ½r²θ, if the question gives an angle in degrees, it must be converted to radians first — otherwise the answer is out by a factor of (180/π).

2Key Features of the sin / cos Graphs

Both sinθ and cosθ are wave-shaped graphs with period 2π and amplitude 1 (maximum value 1, minimum value −1). The two curves are identical in shape, except cosθ is "shifted earlier" by π/2 compared to sinθ — in other words, cosθ = sin(θ + π/2).

sinθ (teal) and cosθ (purple) plotted over 0 to 2π

A few key coordinates are worth memorising, since they act as reference points when solving trig equations:

Key coordinates

sinθ: maximum at θ=π/2 (value=1), minimum at θ=3π/2 (value=−1), zero at θ=0, π, 2π cosθ: maximum at θ=0, 2π (value=1), minimum at θ=π (value=−1), zero at θ=π/2, 3π/2

3Compound Angle Formulae

These formulae let us break down the trig function of a sum or difference of two angles into a combination of trig functions of the individual angles. They form the basis for the double angle formulae and the R sin(θ+α) technique that follow, so understanding the logic matters more than pure memorisation.

sin(A ± B) = sinA cosB ± cosA sinB The sign in the sin expansion matches the sign in the original expression
cos(A ± B) = cosA cosB ∓ sinA sinB Note: the sign in the cos expansion is the OPPOSITE of the original expression!
tan(A ± B) = (tanA ± tanB) / (1 ∓ tanA tanB)
⚠ Common mistake: the middle sign in cos(A+B) is MINUS, and the middle sign in cos(A−B) is PLUS — the exact opposite pattern to sin. This is very easy to mix up; it's worth writing the formula out on scratch paper each time before using it.
Worked example

Find the exact value of sin75° (using sin75° = sin(45°+30°)) sin(45°+30°) = sin45°cos30° + cos45°sin30° = (√2/2)(√3/2) + (√2/2)(1/2) = (√6 + √2) / 4

4Double Angle Formulae

Substituting B=A into the compound angle formulae (i.e. A+A=2A) derives the double angle formulae. This is the best example of "understanding the derivation beats pure memorisation".

sin(2A) = 2 sinA cosA
cos(2A) = cos²A − sin²A = 2cos²A − 1 = 1 − 2sin²A All three forms need to be familiar — exam questions often specify which form to use in a proof
tan(2A) = 2tanA / (1 − tan²A)
Worked example

Simplify: 2sin(3x)cos(3x) Using sin(2A)=2sinAcosA with A=3x: 2sin(3x)cos(3x) = sin(6x)

5R sin(θ+α): The Core Modelling Tool

This is the single most important technique in the whole Trigonometry and Modelling topic. When we meet an expression like a sinθ + b cosθ — two trig functions added together — we can rewrite it as a "single wave" in the form R sin(θ+α). This immediately reveals the amplitude, the maximum/minimum values, and the phase shift.

Deriving the formula

Goal: a sinθ + b cosθ = R sin(θ+α). Expand the right-hand side first:

R sin(θ+α) = R sinθ cosα + R cosθ sinα

Comparing coefficients: the coefficient of sinθ gives a = R cosα; the coefficient of cosθ gives b = R sinα. Squaring both equations and adding:

a² + b² = R²cos²α + R²sin²α = R²(cos²α+sin²α) = R² So R = √(a² + b²)

Dividing the two equations eliminates R:

tanα = b / a Always check which quadrant (a,b) lies in, to make sure α falls in the correct range
y = 3sinθ + 4cosθ and its equivalent form y = 5sin(θ+53.13°) are the exact same curve — R is the amplitude, α is the phase shift

Two key pieces of information can be read directly off the graph:

Reading the graph

Maximum value = R (the highest point on the curve) Location of the maximum: θ + α = 90°, i.e. θ = 90° − α (because sin reaches its maximum value of 1 when its input is 90°, giving R×1 = R)

⚠ Common mistake: when asked for the θ where the maximum occurs, many students write θ=90° directly and forget to subtract α — the correct answer is θ = 90° − α (or in radians: θ = π/2 − α).

6Real-World Modelling: A Tide-Height Model

Modelling questions typically apply the R sin(θ+α) technique to a real-world context — tide heights, Ferris wheel heights, daylight hours, and so on. The key is to simplify the given function into R sin(θ+α) form first; only then can questions about "maximum", "minimum", and "when it occurs" be answered.

Worked scenario

The tide height h(t) (metres) in a bay, t hours after midnight, is modelled by: h(t) = 12 + 5sin(t) − 12cos(t), 0 ≤ t < 24 Step 1: simplify 5sin(t) − 12cos(t) into R sin(t−α) form R = √(5²+12²) = 13 tanα = 12/5 → α = 67.38° So h(t) = 12 + 13sin(t − 67.38°) Step 2: answer the questions Maximum height = 12 + 13 = 25 metres This occurs when t − 67.38° = 90° (converting units as the question requires), giving t ≈ 2.74 hours, i.e. around 2:44 am

Tide height h(t) over 24 hours, showing the maximum, minimum, and the baseline (midline = 12)
⚠ Common mistake: the constant term (12 in this example) is NOT part of the amplitude R — it simply shifts the whole curve vertically. Many students mistakenly calculate the maximum as just "R" and forget to add the constant back in.

📌 Key Takeaways

  • Radians underpin everything — all standard trig derivative results only hold in radian measure, so always check the units before starting a question.
  • Learn the sign rules for compound angle formulae cold: the sin expansion keeps the original sign; the cos expansion flips it.
  • There are three forms of cos(2A) — choose the correct one based on what the question requires (e.g. eliminating sin² or cos²), rather than picking one at random.
  • R sin(θ+α) is the heart of this whole topic: R = √(a²+b²), tanα = b/a (always check the quadrant). The moment you see "a sinθ + b cosθ", your first instinct should be "convert this to R sin(θ+α)".
  • Remember to subtract α when finding where the maximum occurs: θ = 90° − α, not simply 90°.
  • In modelling questions, the constant term (vertical shift) is not part of the amplitude R — maximum = constant + R, minimum = constant − R.
  • The link to Differentiation: the R sin(θ+α) form often lets you read off the maximum/minimum directly without differentiating at all — recognising this shortcut in an exam saves significant time.
  • "Show that" questions require every algebraic step to be written out, not just the final answer — method marks are earned through the steps.
